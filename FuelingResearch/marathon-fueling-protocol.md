## Executive Summary

This document outlines an evidence-based marathon fueling strategy for two athletes training under the Pfitzinger 18/55 program. The protocol targets **dual-transporter carbohydrate saturation** using a 2:1 maltodextrin:fructose blend, with progressive gut training to achieve intake rates of **85–105 g/hr (Aaron)** and **75–95 g/hr (Kristin)**.

The strategy is informed by:
- Current sports nutrition research on carbohydrate oxidation rates
- Comparative analysis of recommendations from multiple AI research agents
- Personal race-day experience and lessons learned
- Physiological principles of gut adaptation and gastric emptying

**Core Principles:**
- Train the gut alongside the legs
- Smaller, more frequent doses over large boluses
- Match fueling practice to race-pace intensity
- Individualize based on GI tolerance and sensitivity

## The Science of Carbohydrate Absorption

### Dual-Transporter Physiology

The human intestine absorbs carbohydrates through two primary transport mechanisms:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    INTESTINAL ABSORPTION                        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                 │
│   SGLT1 Transporter                 GLUT5 Transporter          │
│   ─────────────────                 ─────────────────          │
│   • Absorbs: Glucose,               • Absorbs: Fructose        │
│     Maltodextrin                                               │
│   • Location: Small intestine       • Location: Small intestine│
│   • Capacity: ~60 g/hr              • Capacity: ~30-50 g/hr    │
│   • Sodium-dependent                • Facilitated diffusion    │
│                                                                 │
│   Combined Maximum: 90-120+ g/hr (with training)               │
│                                                                 │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Why Two Transporters Matter

Using only glucose/maltodextrin saturates the SGLT1 transporter at ~60 g/hr. Any excess glucose:
- Accumulates in the intestinal lumen
- Draws water into the gut (osmotic effect)
- Causes bloating, cramping, and GI distress

By adding fructose, we engage a **second, independent pathway** (GLUT5), allowing total absorption rates of 90–120 g/hr—nearly double single-source carbohydrate intake.

### The Trainability of Gut Absorption

Critical insight: **Intestinal transporters upregulate with repeated exposure.**

Research demonstrates that athletes who consistently practice high-carb intake during training:
- Increase SGLT1 and GLUT5 transporter density
- Improve gastric emptying rates
- Reduce GI symptoms at equivalent carb loads
- Achieve higher oxidation rates of exogenous carbohydrates

This adaptation takes **2–4 weeks of consistent practice** to manifest, which is why gut training must begin early in the training cycle.

## Why High-Carb Fueling Works

### The Metabolic Reality of Marathon Running

At marathon intensity (65–80% VO₂max), carbohydrate is the **dominant fuel source**. The body's glycogen stores are finite:

| Storage Site | Capacity | Depletion Timeline |
|--------------|----------|-------------------|
| Muscle glycogen | ~300-400g | 90-120 min at marathon pace |
| Liver glycogen | ~80-100g | Continuously released to maintain blood glucose |
| Blood glucose | ~4-5g | Rapidly depleted without intake |

**The math is simple:** A 3:10 marathon requires ~2,800–3,200 kcal. Glycogen provides ~1,600–2,000 kcal. The deficit must come from exogenous carbohydrates or fat oxidation (which is too slow to maintain pace).

### Carbohydrate Oxidation Rates

| Intake Rate | Single-Source (Glucose Only) | Dual-Source (Glucose + Fructose) |
|-------------|------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| 60 g/hr | ~1.0 g/min oxidation | ~1.0 g/min oxidation |
| 90 g/hr | ~1.0 g/min (excess unabsorbed) | ~1.5 g/min oxidation |
| 120 g/hr | GI distress likely | ~1.75 g/min oxidation |

**Key insight:** Dual-source carbohydrate blends don't just absorb faster—they **oxidize faster**, meaning more fuel reaches working muscles.

### Performance Benefits

Studies consistently show that high-carb fueling (≥90 g/hr) compared to moderate intake (≤60 g/hr):
- Improves marathon performance by **2–5%**
- Reduces perceived exertion in final miles
- Maintains pace better in miles 18–26
- Reduces muscle glycogen depletion

For a 3:10 target, a 3% improvement is **~5-6 minutes**—the difference between goal achievement and falling short.

## The 2:1 Maltodextrin:Fructose Protocol

### Why 2:1?

The 2:1 ratio is designed to **match transporter capacities**:

```
At 90 g/hr intake:
├── Maltodextrin: 60g (saturates SGLT1 at ~60g capacity)
└── Fructose: 30g (within GLUT5 ~30-50g capacity)

Result: Both transporters working at capacity, minimal overflow
```

### When to Adjust the Ratio

| Intake Rate | Recommended Ratio | Reasoning |
|-------------|-------------------|-----------|
| 75–90 g/hr | 2:1 | Optimal SGLT1/GLUT5 balance |
| 90–100 g/hr | 2:1 to 1.5:1 | May benefit from slightly more fructose |
| 100–110 g/hr | 1.2:1 to 1:0.8 | Prevents SGLT1 overflow; utilizes full GLUT5 capacity |

**For Aaron at 100+ g/hr:** Consider shifting toward 1.2:1 or 1:0.8 ratio. At 105 g/hr with 2:1, he'd consume 70g maltodextrin (exceeding SGLT1 capacity) and only 35g fructose (leaving GLUT5 underutilized).

**For Kristin at 75–95 g/hr:** Stay with 2:1. Her target range sits perfectly within the absorption sweet spot.

### DIY Gel Advantages

| Factor | DIY 2:1 Gel | Commercial Gels (e.g., Precision 90) |
|--------|-------------|--------------------------------------|
| Osmolality | ~250–350 mOsm/kg | ~2,000–2,700 mOsm/kg |
| Gastric emptying | Fast | Slower (requires more water) |
| Cost | ~$0.15–0.25/serving | ~$3–4/serving |
| Customization | Full control | Fixed formulation |
| GI tolerance | Generally better | Higher distress risk |

### Recipe Formulation

For every **30g of carbohydrates**:
- Maltodextrin: 20g
- Fructose: 10g
- Citric acid: 0.12g (palatability)
- Sodium (as citrate or table salt): ~300mg
- Water: 30–40ml (adjust for desired consistency)

**Sodium citrate vs. table salt:** Sodium citrate is preferred for high-carb loads. The citrate molecule buffers gut pH, potentially reducing nausea, and the salty-sour taste cuts sweetness better than NaCl.

## Progressive Gut-Training Schedule

### Philosophy

Gut training follows the same progressive overload principles as physical training:
- Increase dose by **~5 g/hr every 2–3 weeks**
- Hold steady if GI symptoms appear
- Maintain dose on cutback/recovery weeks to reinforce adaptation
- **Never increase training volume and fueling dose simultaneously**

### 18-Week Protocol

| Phase | Weeks | Long Run | Aaron (g/hr) | Kristin (g/hr) | Focus |
|-------|-------|----------|--------------|----------------|-------|
| **Baseline** | 1–2 | 12–13 mi | 85 | 75 | Establish timing logistics |
| | 3–4 | 13–15 mi | 85 | 75 | Consistent practice |
| **Build** | 5–6 | 15–16 mi | 90 | 80 | First increment |
| | 7–8 | 16–17 mi | 95 | 85 | Introduce race-pace fueling |
| **Peak** | 9–10 | 17–18 mi | 95 | 85 | Hold; refine timing |
| | 11–12 | 18–20 mi | 100 | 90 | Peak gut stress; dress rehearsals |
| **Sharpen** | 13–14 | 17–20 mi | 100–105 | 90 | Race simulation runs |
| | 15–16 | 12–15 mi | 100–105 | 90–95 | Lock in race-day protocol |
| **Taper** | 17 | 8–12 mi | 100 | 90 | Maintain frequency |
| **Race** | 18 | 26.2 mi | 100–105 | 90–95 | Execute |

### Critical Training Sessions

**Weeks 10–14: Race-Pace Fueling Practice**

Include 2–3 long runs with **race-pace segments (4–6 miles)** while fueling at target rate:
- Aaron: 4–6 miles at 7:15–7:20/mi while taking gels
- Kristin: 4–6 miles at 7:50–7:55/mi while taking gels

**Why this matters:** Training runs are typically 30–60 sec/mi slower than race pace. At slower paces, blood flow to the gut is adequate for digestion. At race pace, splanchnic blood flow decreases significantly. You must practice fueling under race-level gut stress.

## Lessons from Previous Race Experience

### What Happened (Aaron's 3:38 Marathon)

- **Fuel used:** Three Precision 90 gels (270g available, ~202g consumed)
- **Intake rate:** ~55–56 g/hr
- **Timing:** Every ~3 miles (24–26 minutes)
- **Symptom:** Strong pressure/cramping under xiphoid process at miles 18–21
- **Result:** Lost desire to fuel; finished with remaining gel unused

### Root Cause Analysis

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    WHY THE XIPHOID PRESSURE OCCURRED            │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                 │
│   Primary Factors:                                              │
│   ────────────────                                              │
│   1. GASTRIC POOLING                                            │
│      • Race intensity reduced gut blood flow                    │
│      • Gels sat in stomach longer than in training              │
│      • Created distension pressing against diaphragm            │
│                                                                 │
│   2. INADEQUATE FLUID                                           │
│      • Precision 90 gels are hyperosmolar (~2,700 mOsm/kg)      │
│      • Without sufficient water, drew fluid INTO stomach        │
│      • Increased volume and pressure                            │
│                                                                 │
│   3. LARGE BOLUS + LONG GAPS                                    │
│      • 90g gels every 24-26 min = peaks of gastric load         │
│      • Better: 25-30g every 15-20 min = steady absorption       │
│                                                                 │
│   4. DIAPHRAGM FATIGUE                                          │
│      • 2+ hours of elevated breathing rate                      │
│      • Distended stomach pressed upward                         │
│      • Exacerbated discomfort at xiphoid attachment point       │
│                                                                 │
│   Secondary Contributors:                                       │
│   ──────────────────────                                        │
│   • Race-day adrenaline suppressed digestion                    │
│   • Cumulative dehydration thickened gastric contents           │
│   • Late-race posture degradation compressed upper abdomen      │
│   • Intensity exceeded any training fueling practice            │
│                                                                 │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### The Critical Insight

**The failure wasn't too much fuel—it was suboptimal delivery mechanics.**

At 55–56 g/hr, intake was actually *below* the threshold for gut training benefits. The issue was:
- Large doses infrequently delivered
- At an intensity the gut hadn't practiced
- With a hyperosmolar gel requiring more water than consumed

### Protocol Adjustments Based on This Experience

| Previous Approach | Revised Protocol | Reasoning |
|-------------------|------------------|-----------|
| 90g gels every 24–26 min | 25–30g every 15–20 min | Smaller boluses = less gastric pooling |
| Unknown water intake | 100–150ml with every gel | Maintains osmotic balance |
| No race-pace fueling practice | 2–3 sessions at goal pace | Trains gut at reduced blood flow |
| Precision 90 gels (2,700 mOsm/kg) | DIY gel (~300 mOsm/kg) | Lower osmolality = faster emptying |
| No contingency plan | Step-by-step response protocol | Prepared for mid-race issues |

## Troubleshooting & Contingency Protocols

### If Xiphoid Pressure Appears Mid-Race

| Step | Action | Rationale |
|------|--------|-----------|
| 1 | **Stand tall** | Lifts diaphragm off stomach |
| 2 | **5 deep belly breaths** | Expands diaphragm, releases pressure |
| 3 | **Sip water only for 10–15 min** | Dilutes gastric contents without adding load |
| 4 | **Skip one gel dose** | Allows stomach to empty |
| 5 | **Reassess at next mile marker** | If improved: resume with half-dose (15g) |
| 6 | **If pressure persists** | Water only for remainder; rely on glycogen reserves |

### GI Symptom Response Matrix

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Immediate Action | Prevention |
|---------|--------------|------------------|------------|
| Upper abdominal pressure | Gastric distension | Stand tall, deep breaths, water only | Smaller doses, more frequent |
| Nausea | Osmotic stress or intensity | Slow pace slightly, sip water | Better hydration with gels |
| Side stitch | Diaphragm spasm | Exhale forcefully on opposite foot strike | Breathing practice in training |
| Bloating | Carb malabsorption | Reduce dose, extend intervals | More gut training needed |
| Urgent bowel need | Osmotic diarrhea | Find facilities; reduce fructose | Test tolerance in training |

### Late-Race Fueling Decision Tree

```
Mile 20 Assessment:
├── GI feels good?
│   └── YES → Continue fueling every 15-20 min through mile 23
│   └── NO → See below
│
├── Mild discomfort?
│   └── Skip one dose, water only, reassess at mile 22
│
├── Moderate discomfort?
│   └── Water only for remainder; you have enough glycogen for 10K
│
└── Severe distress?
    └── Slow pace, walk aid stations, prioritize finishing over time
```

### Weather Adjustments

| Condition | Fluid Adjustment | Carb Adjustment | Sodium Adjustment |
|-----------|------------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Cool (<50°F) | 400–500 ml/hr | No change | 500 mg/hr |
| Moderate (50–65°F) | 500–600 ml/hr | No change | 600 mg/hr |
| Warm (65–75°F) | 600–800 ml/hr | Consider -5 g/hr | 700–800 mg/hr |
| Hot (>75°F) | 700–1000 ml/hr | Reduce by 10 g/hr | 800–1000 mg/hr |

**Rationale:** In heat, blood flow prioritizes skin for cooling, further reducing gut perfusion. Lower carb targets to match reduced absorption capacity.

### Recovery Fueling

| Timing | Target | Purpose |
|--------|--------|---------|
| 0–30 min post-race | 1.0–1.2 g/kg carbs + 0.3 g/kg protein | Rapid glycogen replenishment |
| 2–4 hours post-race | Normal balanced meal | Continue recovery |
| 24–48 hours post-race | Carb-rich diet | Full glycogen restoration |

## Summary: Key Takeaways

### The Science
- Dual-transporter carbohydrate absorption enables 90–120 g/hr intake
- 2:1 maltodextrin:fructose optimizes SGLT1/GLUT5 utilization up to ~95 g/hr
- Gut adaptation is trainable with consistent practice
- Race intensity reduces gut blood flow—practice accordingly

### The Strategy
- **Aaron:** 85→105 g/hr, start at 0:15, aggressive front-loading
- **Kristin:** 75→95 g/hr, start at 0:20–0:25, graduated dosing
- Both: Every 15–20 min, 25–30g max per dose, 100–150ml water with every gel

### The Execution
- Progressive overload over 18 weeks
- Race-pace fueling practice in weeks 10–14
- DIY gel (low osmolality) preferred over commercial options
- Contingency protocols for mid-race GI distress

### The Lesson
Your previous race experience (xiphoid pressure at mile 18–21) wasn't a ceiling—it was a signal that delivery mechanics needed refinement. With proper training, smaller doses, better hydration, and race-pace gut conditioning, your system can handle 85–105 g/hr.

**The hard part is done: you identified the problem. Now execute the fix.**
